# 56% of Companies Get Zero Return From AI. That's the Best News I've Heard All Year.

*The productivity paradox isn't evidence that AI doesn't work. It's evidence that almost nobody knows how to use it.*

---

Fifty-six percent. That's the share of companies reporting zero return on their AI investments, according to PwC's latest survey. Not negative return — *zero*. As in: we spent the money, deployed the tools, and got nothing back.

Fortune and NBER surveyed 6,000 CEOs and CFOs. The verdict: AI has had "virtually no impact" on productivity. Harvard Business Review and UC Berkeley went further — they found AI isn't just failing to reduce work, it's actually *increasing* workload. Sixty-two percent of junior employees report burnout from AI-related task changes.

Most people read these numbers and think: the AI bubble is popping.

I read them and think: being someone who actually knows how to use AI just became incredibly valuable.

## The Contradiction Nobody's Talking About

Here's what makes this moment so strange. At the exact same time that executives are reporting zero return, AI deployment is accelerating faster than any enterprise technology in history.

Gartner says enterprise agent integration grew 800% year-over-year — from under 5% to roughly 40% of enterprise applications. SoftBank deployed 2.5 million agents across its workforce in ten weeks. That's 100 agents per employee. OpenAI just closed the largest private funding round ever — $100 billion at an $830 billion valuation.

So companies are deploying AI at unprecedented speed *and* reporting that it doesn't work. Both things are true simultaneously. How?

The temptation is to pick a side. Either AI is transformative and the surveys are wrong, or AI is a bubble and the investment is irrational. But I think the Japanese business press, of all places, nailed the real answer.

## The Polarization Thesis

JBpress — one of Japan's leading business publications — declared 2026 "the year of AI polarization." Their argument is more nuanced than anything I've seen from Western analysts: **AI isn't failing. It's splitting the world in two.**

The companies getting zero return are the ones that bolted an AI button onto their existing SaaS tools and called it transformation. Copilot in your email. A chatbot on your help page. An AI summary feature nobody asked for. This is what most of that 56% looks like — cosmetic AI layered on top of workflows that were designed for humans.

The companies getting enormous return — and they exist, they're just a minority — are the ones that redesigned their workflows from scratch around what AI can actually do. Not "AI-assisted" versions of old processes. Entirely new processes that wouldn't make sense without AI.

That's a very different claim than "AI works" or "AI doesn't work," and it has very different implications.

If AI's value follows a power law distribution — where a small number of users capture most of the value — then the 56% figure isn't a death sentence for AI. It's a *sorting mechanism*. It's telling us who knows how to use this technology and who doesn't.

## We've Been Here Before

In 1987, the economist Robert Solow made an observation that became famous: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Companies had been buying PCs for a decade. The productivity gains were nowhere to be found.

It took another ten years for the internet to prove Solow wrong. And the lesson wasn't that computers suddenly got better. The lesson was that organizations finally learned to *reorganize around them*. Economists later called these "complementary innovations" — the process redesigns, organizational changes, and new workflows that actually unlock a technology's value.

The technology was never the bottleneck. The workflow was.

I think we're living through the AI version of the Solow paradox right now. The 56% who report zero return haven't reorganized. They've installed AI the way companies in 1990 installed PCs — putting them on every desk without rethinking what people actually do at those desks.

The difference this time is speed. The internet took a decade to prove Solow wrong. AI iterates faster, and the gap between "deployed" and "effective" is more visible. I'd give it three to five years before the productivity stats catch up — but only for the organizations that do the workflow redesign work.

## SoftBank's 2.5 Million Agents Prove the Point

I keep coming back to the SoftBank number. Two and a half million agents in ten weeks. One hundred per employee.

That sounds impressive until you ask: doing what? The deployment itself tells you nothing about effectiveness. It's the AI equivalent of installing Slack in every department and declaring your company has "transformed communication." The tool is there. But has anyone redesigned how decisions get made? How information flows? How work actually gets done?

I doubt it. Not because SoftBank is incompetent — they're one of the most aggressive AI adopters on the planet — but because workflow redesign is genuinely hard. It requires someone to sit down and think: what should this process look like if AI is a first-class participant, not just an add-on?

That question is a *design* question, not an engineering question. And most organizations don't have people whose job it is to answer it.

## Why This Is a Builder's Moment

I'm a product designer. I don't write code. But I ship AI products every week — PM-Quill (a structured decision workflow for product managers), GBaseGTM (an AI-powered Japanese company database), a modular skill system for agent capabilities.

None of these products are technically impressive by engineering standards. What they do is define *how AI should be used* for a specific task. PM-Quill doesn't give agents more capability. It gives them a structured four-step process: spec, feasibility, plan, review. Each step is designed so the human stays in control and the AI does what it's good at.

That's the complementary innovation. Not better models. Not more agents. Better workflows.

And if 56% of companies are getting zero return because nobody designed the workflow — then there's an enormous, unsaturated market for people who can. This isn't about building AI. It's about designing how AI gets used. It's product design, information architecture, workflow engineering. Skills that product managers, designers, and operations people already have.

Nature published a paper this week arguing that current LLMs constitute AGI. Meanwhile, 6,000 CEOs say they feel nothing. The gap between what AI *can* do and what organizations *actually get* from it is the largest it's ever been. And the people who close that gap — the people who design the workflows, define the tasks, and restructure how work happens — are the scarcest resource in the entire AI economy.

The 56% number isn't AI's obituary. It's a hiring ad for workflow designers.

I happen to be one. And I think there should be a lot more of us.
